Brand strategy / 3 minute read

Brand strategy or a new logo: what does your business need?

Identify whether the problem is the message, the visual identity, or both before commissioning the work.

Brand Blueprint

A new logo can change how a business looks. Positioning changes how the business explains its offer and why someone should choose it. Both can matter, but they solve different parts of the problem. Start by identifying what your customers and your own team are struggling to understand.

Look for the question behind the request.

“We need a refresh” might mean the visuals feel dated. It might also mean the offer has changed, the company is serving a different audience, or every sales conversation starts with a long explanation.

Write down what should become easier after the work. If the answer is “people should understand what we do and who it is for,” positioning and messaging deserve attention before visual execution.

Check whether the offer is easy to explain.

Ask a few people close to the business to describe the priority customer, the problem you solve, and the reason to choose your offer. Differences can reveal decisions that a logo brief alone cannot settle.

A useful strategy brings those decisions together in a positioning statement, value proposition, message hierarchy, and voice guidance. These give later writing and design work a shared direction.

  • Who are we prioritizing?
  • What are they trying to decide or achieve?
  • What do we offer them?
  • Which reasons to believe can we actually support?
  • What should we emphasize first?

Assess the visual system on its own terms.

If the message is settled but the identity is inconsistent, hard to use, or poorly suited to the business, visual identity work may be the next need. That could involve a logo, typography, colors, photography, or application guidelines.

Write those requirements into a separate design brief. A strategy document can inform visual decisions, but the strategy deliverable and the identity production should each have an explicit scope.

Connect the decisions to the next 90 days.

A positioning exercise is more useful when the business knows where the message will appear next. Choose the service introduction, content themes, and campaign brief that need attention first.

For example, the fictional Northline concept starts with the owner’s growth decision, then uses that positioning to shape a headline and content themes. The sample shows how the reasoning informs the writing. It does not represent a completed client engagement.

Choose a scope that matches the problem.

Brand Blueprint covers stakeholder input, a competitor review, one priority buyer profile, positioning, voice guidance, three content themes, and a 90-day plan. Logo and visual identity design, naming, photography, video, and website development are separately scoped.

Bring your existing materials and the decisions you need to make. The first conversation should help establish which work is useful now and which work depends on it.
