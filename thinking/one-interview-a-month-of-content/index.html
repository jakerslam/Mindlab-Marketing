How one expert interview becomes a month of content.

A worked example of turning a business conversation into themes, post ideas, and an article.

Useful content often starts with the questions a business answers every week. An interview gives those answers a structure. The writing work is to find the specific insight, explain it in the company’s voice, and give each piece a purpose. Here is an original fictional example using Northline Advisory.

Ask about decisions, not just topics.

A question such as “What should we post about growth?” usually produces a list of broad themes. More useful prompts ask what customers are deciding, what they misunderstand, and what information changes the conversation.

For the fictional Northline interview, the central prompt is: “What do owners overlook before adding another growth priority?” The sample answer is: “They discuss the new opportunity before deciding what the business can stop doing to make room.” This is an invented source excerpt for the demonstration.

Find the idea that can support several pieces.

The useful idea is the tradeoff behind a growth decision. It can support a short explanation, a practical question, a checklist, and a longer article without repeating the same paragraph.

Keep a source note for each angle so the writer and reviewer can trace the idea back to the conversation. Check the meaning with the expert before adding claims or examples they did not provide.

  • Readiness: is the business able to support the new priority?
  • Tradeoffs: what must pause to make room?
  • Next steps: what can the owner learn before committing?

Give each post a different job.

An eight-post writing plan can combine explanation, practical prompts, and a route to a deeper article. The format should follow the point, rather than forcing every post into the same template. This sample plan demonstrates the structure; it is not a completed monthly delivery.

  • 1. Explain why every new priority creates a tradeoff.
  • 2. Ask what the business would pause to make room.
  • 3. Share a three-question readiness checklist.
  • 4. Explain the difference between an opportunity and a priority.
  • 5. Describe an assumption an owner can test first.
  • 6. Answer a common objection to narrowing the plan.
  • 7. Introduce the longer article with its most useful question.
  • 8. Invite readers to review one decision using the checklist.

Build one article around the full explanation.

A proposed article, “Before your next growth move, name the tradeoff,” can expand the same idea: describe the competing priorities, explain the capacity question, work through a fictional example, and end with a short decision checklist.

This gives the article a different purpose from the posts. It develops the reasoning and provides a useful resource to link to when the same question comes up again.

Review the batch before publishing.

Check accuracy, voice, examples, and the action each piece asks the reader to take. Collect feedback through one approval route so the writing can be revised consistently.

Content Studio includes a monthly source interview, eight written social posts for one profile or page, and one article up to 1,200 words or one email up to 800 words. The editorial calendar and review cycle connect those pieces. Graphics, community management, and email distribution are separately scoped.
